# The nine components

The learnings of an ad live in these nine components. Describe each one in a sentence, quote the line
that carries it, and say whether it is strong, weak or missing. The worked example at the end shows a
full breakdown.

Contents: 1 Angle · 2 Avatar · 3 Desire · 4 Mechanism · 5 Authority · 6 Awareness level · 7 Emotion ·
8 Positioning · 9 Urgency · Worked example

## 1. Angle
**The main reason you give someone to buy.** One sentence: "This will …".
- Find it by asking what the viewer is promised if they buy.
- Levers: switch to a different outcome of the same product (stop the smell → keep the machine clean
  and mold-free → save buying a new machine), or a different problem it solves.
- A new angle usually needs a matching landing page (see `recipes.md`).

## 2. Avatar
**Who the ad speaks to, described by experiences, behaviors and emotions.** Demographics matter only
when they change the story ("has a dog" matters, "42, married, California" does not).
- **Experiences:** what has happened to them ("clothes musty for months", "house smells like damp fur").
- **Behaviors:** what they are doing about it now ("switched detergent", "vinegar runs", "extra
  rinse").
- **Emotions:** how they feel about it ("frustrated", "grossed out", "embarrassed").
- Sources: Reddit threads, YouTube and TikTok comments, product reviews (including competitors'),
  customer emails. Copy their exact words. That makes copy feel like mind reading.
- Avatars exist on a scale. The less aware the ad, the broader the avatar can be (anyone with a
  washing machine), and the better the copy has to be.

## 3. Desire
**The core want the ad taps.** It comes from Breakthrough Advertising (Eugene Schwartz): you cannot
create desire, only channel an existing one. Ask "why?" until you reach an instinct. ("I want a clean
machine" → why? → "dirty laundry is a health hazard" → health.)
- Common core desires: health, status (what others think of you), money and savings, comfort and
  convenience, safety and security, belonging and family, attraction, time and freedom, self-image.
- Desire switch: keep the story and change what is at stake. Health → status: "my co-workers asked
  whether my clothes always smell like this."

## 4. Mechanism
**How the product actually works.** Customers do not care about the ingredient; they care what it
does for them. Tie every part of the mechanism to a benefit:
`<ingredient or feature> + <what it does> + <so that you get…>`.
- Example: "Protease breaks down the protein in pet hair, so the hair finally rinses out."
- Explaining the mechanism clearly can carry an account. One brand went from about $3k to $30k a day
  in spend with ads that only explained how the product delivers the result.
- The authority should recommend the mechanism, not the brand ("he said the only thing that works is
  enzymes, and this tablet is the only one with all three").

## 5. Authority
**The reason people believe the ad.** The most important component. "Learn to make people believe your
claims. If they believe, they buy." One community member doubled from $50k to $100k a day within a
week after an iteration with an authority switch that took $15k a day in spend.

Kinds of authority, from strong to weak:
- **A trusted third person:** a profession or experience that knows the problem (a grandfather who
  repaired washing machines, a technician, a laundromat owner). They tell what is wrong, not the brand.
- **Demonstration and proof:** a test on camera, UV light, a teardown, before and after, lab numbers.
- **Format authority:** a list of "common mistakes" or an educational how-to makes the viewer expect
  to learn something, which creates an authority bias on its own.
- **Social proof:** real customer counts, ratings, reviews, press.
- **Origin story of the mechanism:** where the technology comes from, only if true.

Rules: the authority must be true, or a story clearly told as a story. Never invent an expert,
doctor, review or number. Authority switches are the single best iteration lever. Try them early.

## 6. Awareness level
**Where the ad starts the viewer** (Breakthrough Advertising):

| Level | The viewer… | Typical opening |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | doesn't know they have the problem | "Did you know…?", a shocking fact, "How your … is secretly …" |
| Problem aware | knows the problem, not the solutions | Agitate: "This will be uncomfortable to hear…" |
| Solution aware | knows solutions exist, tried some | List what they tried and why it failed |
| Product aware | knows your kind of product | Mechanism and positioning: why this one |
| Most aware | knows you, needs a reason now | Offer, guarantee, urgency |

- Agitating the problem makes an ad problem aware. Listing failed solutions makes it solution aware.
- Lower awareness reaches a broader audience. The easiest way down is to start with "Did you know…?".
- Moving a winner up or down one level is a strong iteration.

## 7. Emotion
**What the ad makes people feel and how hard.** Two axes:
- **Valence:** pleasant (relief, pride, joy) to unpleasant (disgust, shame, fear).
- **Intensity:** low ("it smells a bit") to high ("you're slow cooking your clothes in mold").

| | Low intensity | High intensity |
|---|---|---|
| Unpleasant | "My towels smell a bit musty" | "You're slow cooking your clothes in mold" |
| Pleasant | "Fresh laundry again" | "She finally slept with her blanket again" |

Turning up intensity on a working ad is a cheap iteration: the same body with a more intense hook.

## 8. Positioning
**Why you and not everyone else.** Positioning plus mechanism is what people call a unique
mechanism; keep them apart in the card because they can be changed separately.
- Be different rather than better. A red greens powder in a market of green ones is not better, but it
  stands out.
- Patterns: "the first and only …", "made for …" (a specific avatar), a named enemy ("the
  100-year-old bleach formula"), a category of one.
- Only claim "first", "only" or "best" when it is true and you can show it.

## 9. Urgency
**Why now and not later.** Authentic urgency, not "sale ends tonight" on an ad that runs for weeks.
- **Consequence urgency:** the problem gets worse or is harmful now ("you're slow cooking your clothes
  in mold"). The strongest kind.
- **Story-tied scarcity:** "people are realizing how dirty their machines are, so it keeps selling
  out", but only if true.
- **Real offer deadlines:** a dated campaign or a stock limit that really exists.
- Moving the urgency line to the very start of the ad is a proven iteration.

## Worked example: a washing machine cleaner

| Component | Original ad | Iteration 1 | Iteration 2 (the brand's top ad) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Angle | Fixes bad-smelling laundry | Same | Keeps your machine clean and mold-free |
| Avatar | Pet owner whose machine smells | Broad: anyone who never cleans the machine | Pet owner |
| Desire | Health | Status ("co-workers asked if my clothes always smell like this") | Health |
| Mechanism | 3 enzymes, each tied to a benefit | Same | Same |
| Authority | Grandpa who repaired washing machines | Same grandpa, more prominent | The "3 common mistakes" list format |
| Awareness | Solution aware (lists failed fixes) | Problem aware ("this will be uncomfortable to hear") | Unaware ("did you know") |
| Emotion | Unpleasant, low intensity | Same | Unpleasant, high intensity |
| Positioning | First and only enzyme cleaning tablet | Same | Same |
| Urgency | "You're slow cooking your clothes in mold" | "It's selling out as people realize" | The mold line moved to the first second |

What was inherited: iteration 1 kept the grandpa authority; iteration 2 kept the mold urgency and
moved it to the front. Both became breakthroughs.
